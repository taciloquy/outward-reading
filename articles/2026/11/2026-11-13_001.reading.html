千禧一代 vs 婴儿潮：一场正在撕裂美国的代际战争

日期：2026-11-13 分类：美国《华盛顿邮报》外刊讲解 第1227天 阅读时长：约 10 分钟

Washington Post · 标题
The Return of Generational Warfare
Paragraph 1
As last week’s midterms demonstrated, young people appear poised to drive American politics, and particularly the Democratic Party, further to the left. This is particularly true in big cities. New York Mayor Zohran Mamdani was elected last year with crucial support from younger voters, while in Los Angeles, preelection surveys showed democratic socialist (and now Mayor-elect) Nithya Raman leading by almost five to one among voters in their thirties and by more than four to one among voters aged 18 to 29.
Paragraph 2
Nationally, two-thirds of voters aged 18 to 29 voted Democratic in House races last Tuesday. The recent return of generational conflict has a 1960s vibe. As back then, it’s affecting the “rich” countries like the United States, Canada, the U.K., and the nations of the European Union, but it’s also occurring in poorer regions, with mass protests spreading from Paris and Milan to Katmandu, Jakarta, Manila, New Delhi, and Sri Lanka. A new generational war could prove devastating. Societies, noted Aristotle, are like ships: though the crew members have different roles, they all must work together on a common journey.
Paragraph 3
The temptation to go after the successful baby boomers seems irrepressible. Many economists and politicians treat the aging population as a great liability. The United States, like the rest of the world, is older than it has ever been: the population aged 65 and older is projected to grow from 58 million in 2022 to 82 million by 2050. The Atlantic, for example, denounces a corrupt gerontocracy that has “amassed disproportionate wealth and power.”
Paragraph 4
Burning down their grandparents’ world may feel satisfying in the moment, but the notion that older Americans had it easier is not convincing. Boomers faced interest rates as high as 17 percent and unemployment rates much higher than today.
Paragraph 5
Many Generation Z (zoomers) and millennials appear to lack appreciation for the contributions of their elders. In the U.S. alone, according to one financial estimate, younger cohorts will harvest upward of $73 trillion from older generations by 2045.
Paragraph 6
To be sure, youthful resentment has roots in reality: fewer than half of American millennials are doing better than their parents. Between 2022 and 2025, median net worth rose 37 percent for Americans aged 75 and over while falling 23 percent among those under 35. In terms of income, millennials have fared better than their parents but still lag behind in net worth, largely because of low homeownership rates. According to the U.S. Census Bureau, the homeownership rate among young adults aged 25 to 34 was 45 percent for Generation X but dropped to 37 percent for millennials. Not coincidentally, the share of Americans aged 18 to 29 living at home has grown steadily in recent years and now sits at 49 percent. This creates the prospect of a new cohort of “forever renters” dependent on large corporations or the state to supply their residences.
Paragraph 7
Housing is just one source of younger Americans’ skepticism about the future. Today, the angst is more generational, as 69 percent of millennials believe success is much harder to achieve than it was for older generations.
Paragraph 8
Underemployment among recent college graduates has reached 42 percent, and many young people carry massive student-loan debt. One might expect that young people would be excited about unprecedented technological progress, from the rise of space industries to artificial intelligence. Instead, many view the rise of AI and robots with trepidation. According to Pew, 73 percent of Americans under 30 think AI means fewer jobs.
Paragraph 9
Fear of the future under capitalism plays into the hands of those promoting an authoritarian alternative. It’s not just the flag that young people are unenthusiastic about but the liberal economic system that has powered the American experiment. According to a Cato Institution/YouGov poll, 62 percent of Americans under 30 have a positive view of socialism; one-third support Communism. What “socialism” and “capitalism” mean remains up in the air.
Paragraph 10
Of course, despite the talk of geezer greed, not all boomers are rich. Forty percent of U.S. retirees have insufficient savings, and 19 percent have already burned through what they had stored away. The more constructive alternative to generational war, of course, would be a truce. Seniors often support their children financially. In 2024, 2.6 million parents gave over $26 billion to their children living outside the home. Nearly one in four first-time homebuyers rely on families for down payments.
Paragraph 11
Boomers—the vast majority of seniors—and the aging Xers account for the largest group of formal and informal volunteers helping out their neighbors and fellow citizens. Today, more than two-thirds of grandparents are caregivers, often on a regular, even daily basis, contributing over $900 billion to the broader economy by allowing parents to work or go to school. Seniors need the younger generations, too, especially as they age. The truth is, the generations need each other, as they always have. Most young Americans aspire to own a single-family home and start a family. It’s the obligation of the older generation to help them achieve this—not for their own pleasure, but for the future of society.
