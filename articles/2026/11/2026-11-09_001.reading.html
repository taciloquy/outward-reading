一家银行塌了，把拉美这位“明星优等生”一直背着光的那一面，掀到了台前

Financial Times · 标题
A bank collapse exposes the dark side of Latin America’s star economyUeno Bank’s seizure has toppled Paraguay’s president and tarnished its glossy pitch to investors
一家银行塌了，把拉美这位“明星优等生”一直背着光的那一面，掀到了台前
Paragraph 1
The stunning downfall of Paraguay’s president and collapse of its most popular bank have exposed cracks in a free-market success story hailed by investors and the global conservative movement in recent years.
Paragraph 2
The presidency of Santiago Peña, a smooth-talking former IMF economist, unravelled in less than 72 hours this week after regulators seized control of Ueno Bank, a flashy digital lender used by more than half of Paraguayan adults that had become insolvent.
Paragraph 3
Lawmakers quickly launched impeachment proceedings against Peña, a former investor in Ueno’s holding company who installed its boss to lead Paraguay’s central bank and entrusted the lender with hundreds of millions of dollars of public money. The senate approved Peña’s ouster on Friday.
Paragraph 4
The episode, which has left 2.9mn people unable to access their savings, has shocked Paraguayans after their technocratic president spent three years in office promising to build a “modern” state that might one day join the OECD, and travelling the world to promote Paraguay as a haven for foreign capital. “We thought we were improving, but actually we’re the same as always,” said Elizabeth Gonzalez, 28, a barista in a café in downtown Asunción, a few blocks from where hundreds of protesters are maintaining a vigil after larger demonstrations earlier this week.
Paragraph 5
Paraguay has emerged as an oasis of consistent GDP growth and macroeconomic stability within Latin America over the past decade. Peña also secured the country’s first investment-grade credit ratings. But the rapid implosion of Peña’s government, analysts said, would draw attention to deep-rooted problems with corruption and clientelism that have marred Paraguay’s economic development story. “The underbelly of the success story has been an institutional landscape that is . . . captive to a very small number of groups and families,” said Mariano Machado, Americas principal at risk intelligence firm Verisk Maplecroft. “That’s much more visible now than it was at the start of this week.”
Paragraph 6
Paraguay has been ruled by the same party — the rightwing Colorados — for 75 of the last 80 years. Peña’s impeachment was largely the result of a rift with party president Horacio Cartes. Cartes picked Peña as a candidate in 2023 elections. But earlier this year Peña moved to form his own movement within the Colorado Party, sidelining Cartes’s influence. “Peña thought he could walk alone and that Cartes would fade into irrelevance,” said Sebastián Acha, a consultant and former lawmaker in Paraguay. “Cartes did not agree.”
Paragraph 7
The central bank had already received at least 10 warnings since last year regarding Ueno’s substantial lending to entities related to its own parent company and other financial issues, according to Paraguayan newspaper ABC Color. Ueno Bank had a prominent role in Peña’s vision of a modern Paraguayan economy. Founded as a fintech in 2021, it quickly obtained a banking licence and waged an aggressive marketing campaign to bring on new clients, including sponsoring the national football team.
Paragraph 8
Ueno took in more than $850mn in public money, including a tenth of the social security agency’s investment fund, and many small businesses began to rely on its payment system in Paraguay’s heavily informal economy. Ueno founder Miguel Vázquez spoke about becoming the country’s first unicorn and listing on the New York Stock Exchange. “Everything that man said was just window dressing, because it was all a sham,” said Eduardo Lopez, 35, a businessman at the protest who works in exporting goods to the US. “It affected all businesses . . . and it makes the country look bad. It’s a disaster.”
Paragraph 9
In the early hours of Tuesday morning, the central bank froze Ueno accounts and began a process to wind down its operations. The monetary authority said it would guarantee deposits up to the equivalent of about $38,000 per customer and that there was no risk to the wider financial system. The response has raised hopes that Paraguay’s institutions will strengthen in the wake of the Ueno fiasco. “We are seeing the start of corrective measures,” said José Tomás Sánchez, a director at ICA consultancy in Asunción, arguing that the rapid arrest and charging of those involved “sends a very strong signal . . . that you can no longer do this kind of thing in Paraguay.”
Paragraph 10
But others were less optimistic. “It’s pretty clear that this crisis was only resolved because Cartes decided to abandon Peña for political reasons,” said Ramiro Blazquez Giomi, strategist at financial services group Stone X. “It suggests there haven’t been the kind of institutional improvements that we had hoped to see. “We’re suffering the consequences of Peña splitting from Cartes,” said Batarram Caballero, 23, president of the student centre at the National University of Paraguay. “This is a group of people who have meddled and are destroying our country.”
