450万移民改变了美国：他们究竟是谁，又去了哪里？

The Associated Press · 标题
A Border Surge Reshaped America. Now, Data Shows Who 4.5 Million Migrants Were and Where They Went
450万移民改变了美国：他们究竟是谁，又去了哪里？
Paragraph 1
An unprecedented surge in border crossings over the past decade reshaped America: Millions of people from more than 150 countries arrived, bringing the U.S. immigrant population to its highest levels ever. Now, data obtained exclusively by The Associated Press paints a picture of who these migrants are, where they went, and what happened after the border. Blocks, neighborhoods and entire cities were remade in places like Elkhart, Indiana; Knoxville, Tennessee; and Dodge City, Kansas. This was no longer just a story about the border. It was a transformation unfolding in the everyday lives of Americans.
Paragraph 2
Via a Freedom of Information Act request, AP obtained federal records on the self-reported destinations of 4.5 million migrants released in the United States after crossing the Mexican border — both legally and illegally — between October 2018 and July 2025. The Border Patrol encountered migrants from 174 countries in one year alone, a sharp departure from earlier periods when they were nearly all Mexican and Central American. Some went to cities that have long been magnets for immigrants — New York, Miami — but increasingly, others found their way to smaller cities with less developed immigrant footholds.
Paragraph 3
Cincinnati, for example, saw a large number of arrivals from West Africa, Uzbekistan and India. New York City’s Queens borough registered at least 25 nationalities with significant arrivals, including five countries that each sent more than 7,000 people: Ecuador, China, Colombia, Venezuela and India. While Florida was the most popular state overall, Latin American immigrants increasingly looked elsewhere for more promising job prospects and better quality of life. Nearly six of every 10 migrants released at the border were women and children, a dramatic shift from a few years earlier when migrants were predominantly men.
Paragraph 4
In 2023, Samba Diallo and roughly 2,000 of his countrymen arrived in Lockland, Ohio, increasing the town’s population by more than 50%, so suddenly it seemed to some that it happened overnight. No one knew they were coming, and Lockland has no formal services for immigrants. They piled into apartments, sometimes 12 or 15 in a space designed for a single family. They slept on mattresses pressed side by side on the floor. The fire chief warned there had been some small fires — they were unaccustomed to American stoves — and in a crammed apartment complex, this could pose a serious hazard.
Paragraph 5
The influx of immigrants shocked this small town. One evening in 2023, Vincent Wilson, a local bicycle shop owner, arrived to teach an English class at a Christian-run center next to his bike shop. Instead of the group of a dozen or so West Africans he’d known for years, 160 people lined up around the building. Down the street, the lobby at a food and clothing pantry was packed, another line stretching out the door. Wilson realized they were walking miles a day. He put out a request for people to donate old, unused bikes.
Paragraph 6
They didn’t speak much English at first, but Wilson figured out that the language of bicycles is universal: point, grunt, giggle and everyone understands. The Mauritanians became more visible in the community, walking around town, often in groups, sometimes wearing traditional robes unusual on American streets. Wilson became a familiar face to the newcomers, helping them navigate a country whose language and customs were still foreign to them.
Paragraph 7
Khalidou Sy, a well-known hip-hop artist back home, became the Mauritanian community’s unofficial mayor. He spoke at churches and community meetings, explaining who all these new neighbors were. He also did some schooling of his fellow countrymen, he said. Mauritania is a more casual society, and here people strictly follow rules, he remembers scolding. Don’t whistle at women. Cross the street at the crosswalk.
Paragraph 8
Rumors and lies spread. Rumors began circulating that the newcomers to Lockland were eating the ducks in the city park. Right-wing media reported about what was happening here as an “invasion.” KKK flyers started showing up, complaining of “illegal immigrants” and calling on “red-blooded white Americans” to “take your stand.” A group of neo-Nazis arrived from out of town, waving swastikas on a highway overpass. City leaders started sounding the alarm. The West Africans weren’t committing serious crimes, officials told the local media. The major concern was their overcrowded living conditions.
Paragraph 9
Sy feels like he’s part of a great American tradition, the “melting pot,” he said, like the Irish and Italians before him. But he’s yet to put up an American flag his realtor gave him. “I love it, I wish I was American so I could hang it outside, but I cannot pretend,” he said. Maybe one day — his asylum application is still pending. For some Americans, immigration threatens the country they have always known. For newcomers like Sy, it represents the very tradition that made America what it is. The question is whether Americans can still agree on what that identity means.
