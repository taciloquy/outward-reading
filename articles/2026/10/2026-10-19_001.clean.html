El Niño Threatens Global Sugar Supply

Conflict. Climate change. A withering El Niño.

All are conspiring to spoil the world's sweet tooth. Global sugar prices have soared, as a hot, dry summer scorched much of the sugar-producing parts of the world, nudging overall food prices upward.

Nowhere is that felt as acutely as it is in India, the biggest sugar consumer in the world and the second-biggest producer.

The worst monsoon in 11 years and fears of a poor sugarcane harvest have spiked prices amid the Hindu festival season, when gods and mortals alike are feted with sweets.

Making matters worse, high sugar prices are also complicating India's energy security goals.

Sugarcane is a key crop for the country's biofuels push, all the more so as India seeks to curb oil imports while the waterways of the Middle East are choked by war.

This year's El Niño has left many of the country's sugarcane fields parched, just a year after they were drenched by excess rains and devoured by pests. Protests have broken out over rising prices.

The government has warned sugar traders against hoarding. And a country that usually exports sugar has taken the extraordinary step of opening the doors to imports this year, further raising global sugar prices.

"This crop is gone," Morarji Patil, a 30-year-old farmer, said as he stood beside his cane field one sweaty September morning in the Marathwada region of western India.

Like other farmers here, Mr. Patil was already crushed by debt and high fertilizer prices before the blistering heat and scant rains.

His cane stalks were thin and short, stunted by a lack of water this summer when they needed it most. Mr. Patil expected his yields to be 70 percent lower than normal.

India represents an object lesson on the far-reaching effects of conflict and extreme weather on a nation's economy. Food prices are rising month by month.

Key crops remain at risk as the long tail of El Niño drags out through next year, heightening the likelihood of extreme drought and flood. On top of that, India is extremely vulnerable to global oil supply shocks.

It imports the vast majority of its crude oil. It's not just India. Extraordinary heat and drought this year have hit sugarcane in Brazil, the world's biggest sugar producer, and sugar beets in Europe.

Global sugar prices are nearly 15 percent higher than they were a year ago. In India, sugar has long enjoyed state support.

Most recently, the industry profited from a government directive that made 20-percent ethanol blends the standard fuel at Indian gas stations.

The government said its biofuels push reduced oil demand and climate pollution, while also saving the country's precious foreign exchange. It called ethanol blends "a key pillar of India's energy transition." It offered sugar refineries an additional source of revenue.

It also infuriated Indian drivers. Aditya Kare, 21, was refueling his 2018 Hero motorcycle at a gas station in the nearby city of Latur.

He said the ethanol blend had reduced his average mileage from 60 kilometers per liter to around 45, and he was having a hard time making do with the 100 rupees, a little more than $1, that his parents gave him for his commute to college.

"At home, we are planning to get an electric bike," he said. Sugarcane comes with a high cost.

It is a thirsty crop, and its demand, for both food and fuel, is forcing many farmers to drill deeper for water and drain local aquifers. Mr. Patil's well goes down 800 feet.

This year, even at that depth, there is no water. A dam on the Manjara River has become a dry ditch. Ponds are bare.

"This environment is not good for sugarcane," said Ganesh Gomare, a farmer. "But the economy of Marathwada is dependent on sugarcane." "Sugar is tempting. It enjoys relatively high profits."

So Mr. Gomare, 38, devoted more of his land to sugarcane this year. It backfired. He expects his yields to be half of normal.

Nearby, Dagdu Venkat Rao Padile, 66, uprooted a stalk of sugarcane from his field. The plant was emaciated. Its sugar content would be paltry.

"It will cost me more to cut it than what I'll get for it," Mr. Padile said. His yields would be one-fourth of normal. The rains were so poor that he had left most of his land fallow.

Tanisha Gaikwad had all but cut sugar from her diet. But she joined the line at the sweet shop for a small box of laddoos to offer at the altar for Mahalaxmi, a goddess of wealth and power.

Her prayers, she said, could not be sugar-free.
