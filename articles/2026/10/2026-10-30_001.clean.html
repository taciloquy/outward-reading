Spain's Sánchez declares three days of mourning after at least 68 killed in Toledo train crash

The Spanish prime minister has declared three days of mourning after at least 68 people were killed and dozens more injured in a collision between two high-speed trains that officials suggested was caused by an "inexplicable" error at the central command centre.

The trains, one from Spain's state-owned Renfe and the other from the private rail company Iryo, were travelling on Wednesday morning in the same direction on the line that stretches from the Spanish capital, Madrid, to the southern region of Andalusia.

After the Iryo train came to a halt, the other slammed into its rear, knocking several carriages off the track near the town of Urda.

Josimar Medina woke a little before 9am on Wednesday to find himself airborne and hurtling through his carriage on the fast train from Madrid to Seville.

"I remember flying through the air and looking for my girlfriend, who was also shooting through the air," said Medina, a 37-year-old tourist from the northern Peruvian city of Chiclayo.

Once he landed, he looked around and realised just how lucky he and his girlfriend, Vitoria Maia, had been.

"I saw people with cuts, people covered in blood, people missing limbs, and I saw one person who had nothing left below the chest," said Medina.

The Peruvian stood recounting the details of Wednesday's deadly rail crash, in which at least 68 people were killed and dozens more were injured, wearing a light brown T-shirt stained with the blood of a young American man he had helped move to safety.

The couple climbed out of the train and helped the injured while they waited for medics and Guardia Civil officers to arrive at the scene of the crash, which took place amid vineyards and olive groves near the small town of Urda.

Fredrich and Arabella Barilea, a couple from the Philippines who now live in Dubai, were on the Iryo train, and Fredrich was sent flying by the impact. "One guy was shouting: 'Get out! Get out! Another train could hit!'" said Fredrich.

"We saw all the people were also getting off. Many of them had head injuries and were covered in blood. We just got as far away from the train as we could."

"It all happened in a split second," said Arabella. "The table we were sitting at just disappeared in the crash. I feel like we're on our second life."

Spain's transport minister, Óscar Puente, said that rear-end collisions like that which had taken place on Wednesday were "highly unlikely" and suggested that the accident may have been caused by human error made in "inexplicable" circumstances.

He said the LZB signalling system, which automatically halts a train if the line ahead is occupied, had gone down at about 8.30am local time due to a computer malfunction.

As a result, an estimated six trains had been halted along a stretch of around 63km of track, he said. The task of monitoring the rail lines then fell to a 24-hour command centre in Madrid.

Minutes after the computer malfunction, the centre instructed at least two of the trains to continue as normal, despite the fact that there was still a "stop signal" in place, said Puente.

"It was the control centre, through its operators, that made the decision to resume traffic, under circumstances that, from our point of view, are inexplicable," he said.

"Clearly, it was a wrong decision." Giving initial details about the crash, Puente said the Iryo train had been travelling ahead on the tracks and had been sitting stationary when the Renfe train was instructed by the command centre to resume its journey.

The Renfe train then travelled approximately 25km before colliding with the Iryo train about 17 minutes later. The control centre "had more than enough time to verify the error, and they did not fix it", Puente added.

Puente said that similar erroneous instructions had been given to at least one other train on Wednesday morning.

"And it was the engineer of one of the trains who corrected the command centre and told them he can't resume normal operation – it has to be [an] on-sight operation," Puente said.

The tragedy was the second major deadly high-speed train accident this year in Spain, rattling a country where the extensive rail network has long been a point of pride.

In January, 46 people were killed and dozens more injured after two trains – again, one from Renfe and one from Iryo – collided on the same line near the town of Adamuz in Andalucía.

But, as also happened at Adamuz, the solidarity and kindness on display is likely to turn to anger over the coming hours as people seek answers as to how one of Europe's most modern and extensive high-speed rail networks could experience two deadly collisions that have between them claimed at least 114 lives in the space of just 10 months.

The opposition People's party leader, Alberto Núñez Feijóo, called for an immediate investigation into what had happened, as well as a "complete and independent review" of Spain's entire rail network.

"Spain has the right to know what is going on with its infrastructure," he told reporters.
