An International Career Forged by the Struggles of Apartheid

Navi Pillay, who won this year's Nobel Peace Prize, fought racism in South Africa, shaping her international career as a judge and advocate for justice and human rights.

She lived her childhood in a slum without sewage or water, attended a school for only children of Indian descent, and knew that her skin color prohibited her from going to the beach her father would drive past during the Christmas season.

But she also described a pivotal moment that helped her rise from under the thumb of racism: Parents in her community in the coastal city of Durban pooled their money to send her to university, after her high school principal said she had potential. That opportunity began a barrier-breaking career that led Ms. Pillay to becoming a lawyer, a judge on multiple international courts, the human rights chief of the United Nations and, on Friday, a Nobel Peace Prize laureate.

In announcing the prize, the Nobel committee said that Ms. Pillay had been "instrumental in ensuring that war crimes, crimes against humanity and genocide are prosecuted." She becomes the fifth South African to win the award, joining the ranks of the country's most vaunted figures including Nelson Mandela and Archbishop Desmond Tutu.

While much of Ms. Pillay's global recognition comes from human rights work she has done abroad, her experiences in South Africa have indelibly shaped her life, and she has remained deeply invested in her home country's sometimes-fragile democracy. "I see this award as the voice of the majority of the world," Ms. Pillay said in an interview with the South African state broadcaster on Friday. She added that a majority of South Africans "want the rule of law, they want the independent judiciary to be respected."

She studied law at what is now the University of KwaZulu-Natal. Because it was segregated, Black students had to attend classes at night, said Jessica Neuwirth, a human-rights lawyer and activist who has known Ms. Pillay for four decades. During the day, Ms. Pillay would go to the library, where she found books about the Nuremberg trials, helping inspire her fascination with international justice, Ms. Neuwirth said.

When the apartheid government locked up her husband, Gaby Pillay, who was also a lawyer, for political activities and cut off his access to the outside world, Ms. Pillay used his power of attorney to file an action on his behalf, Ms. Neuwirth said. She ended up winning an order prohibiting his torture.

Ms. Pillay's struggle to maneuver within a racist system applying unjust laws in some ways helped her develop the persistence she needed, she has said in interviews. She said in a podcast interview last year that she would keep filing legal motions even if they had little chance of succeeding because she believed that was the only weapon she had to fight the system. "It was clear to me the only thing I can do is keep going, seeking justice," she said.

She rose to international prominence shortly after apartheid ended in the 1990s, as a judge on the tribunal on the Rwanda genocide, which broke new ground in prosecuting the crime of genocide and treating mass rape as a form of genocide. Ms. Pillay later served as a judge on the International Criminal Court, the U.N. High Commissioner for Human Rights, and a substitute judge of the International Court of Justice.

The prize, however, was not welcomed everywhere. Speaking at a rally in Syracuse, New York, on Friday evening, President Trump spent about 10 minutes attacking the decision, describing Ms. Pillay as a "mystery person" he had never heard of and warning Norway that he would not forget it. On social media, he said he deserved eight Nobel Peace Prizes for the wars he claims to have settled, though some of those conflicts continue and others were ended largely by other parties.

The chairman of the Norwegian Nobel Committee replied calmly, saying the panel is independent of the government and bound only by the will of Alfred Nobel. Ms. Pillay, for her part, had already joked to an applauding crowd in Nuremberg that if they kept clapping, she might share the prize with Mr. Trump.
