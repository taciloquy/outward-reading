A hurricane is coming to tax systems

全球富国税制本就千疮百孔，AI又将掀翻以劳动税为核心的根基，一场税收飓风正在逼近
THE YIELD on ten-year US Treasuries has charged through the 5% 
    /jiːld/（债券）收益率；产出/ˈtreʒəriz/（美国）国债
barrier. The spread between French government bonds and German 
             /spred/利差，价差
Bunds is at levels not seen since the euro-zone crisis in 2012.
Britain's newish prime minister has this week set out a plan for 
spending bolstered by a taboo-busting promise to curb 
                                                 /kɜːb/抑制，限制
state-pension increases—and yet is still in a fiscal pickle.
                                              /ˈfɪskl/财政的
Across the rich world, countries are struggling to coax more 
                                                   /kəʊks/诱使，想方设法获取
growth out of their economies. And that's not the worst of it.
As we explain this week, the tax base in all countries, which 
depends heavily on taxing labour, may be upended by artificial 
                                         /ʌpˈendɪd/被颠覆，被彻底改变
intelligence.
Add these problems together, and it is as if a mighty hurricane 
were about to hit the rich world's tax systems—which are 
hopelessly unprepared. The way the state raises money is wasteful
, unfair and perverse.
You would have thought governments would be scrambling to 
strengthen the system before the hurricane makes landfall, but as
                                          （飓风）登陆
ever they are running away from tax reform instead.
The accumulated flaws of modern tax systems are so ubiquitous 
                                                   /juːˈbɪkwɪtəs/无处不在的
that they have become part of the economic backdrop. Taxes are 
full of needless complexity.
In Germany the rate of value-added tax (VAT) on oat milk is more 
than double that on cow's milk, for example. Such widespread 
special treatment has ruinous fiscal consequences.
                      /ˈruːɪnəs/毁灭性的，招致损失的/ˈfɪskl/财政的
Across the OECD, a club of mostly rich countries, consumption 
taxes raise only 58% of what they could if there were no 
carve-outs and no evasion. Tax systems also create perverse 
                  /ɪˈveɪʒn/（税款）逃避，规避
incentives.
When a parent in Britain increases their net income from £99,999 
($131,500) to £100,000, they automatically lose free child care 
worth thousands of pounds. This can prompt some of Britain's most
productive workers to cut their hours or overstuff their pensions
.
And taxes are widely seen as unfair. In Britain the median 
                                                    /ˈmiːdiən/中位数的
employee pays 55% more tax than their self-employed counterparts.
Many people feel the rich aren't doing their bit.
In California voters will decide in November whether to charge a 
one-off 5% levy on residents with wealth of over $1bn. No one 
           /ˈlevi/征收的税款；征税
knows when the fiscal weather will turn. But rapid advances in AI
               /ˈfɪskl/财政的
could accelerate matters.
Taxes on labour are the workhorse of most tax systems worldwide, 
                        主力（≠驮马、勤勤恳恳干活的人）
raising around half of all revenue in the OECD.
In the coming years AI could well displace workers or cut their 
                                  /dɪsˈpleɪs/取代；迫使离开岗位
wages even as corporate profits rise, shrinking labour's share of
GDP. This in turn could reduce revenues and increase benefits 
payments, making deficits even harder to support.
Optimists may hope that superfast growth will make the maths 
easier to handle. Yet The Economist has looked at what would 
happen in rich countries if labour's share of national income 
fell by ten points.
By our calculations, Italy's budget deficit would double as a 
                                    /ˈdefɪsɪt/赤字，逆差
share of GDP and Germany and France would not fare much better. 
In addition, the capacity for tax avoidance would no longer be 
                             避税（合法节税）
limited by human ingenuity.
                 /ˌɪndʒəˈnjuːəti/独创力，机灵才智
Point countless AI agents at Britain's 23,000-page tax code and 
hundreds of new tax-avoidance strategies are likely to emerge—and
don't bet on the Treasury to keep up. What to do?
Given that AI is likely to mint vast fortunes for a select few, 
countries may be inclined to follow California's proposal and 
introduce wealth taxes.
Yet such taxes will probably backfire, either because they are 
                             /ˌbækˈfaɪə/事与愿违，产生反效果
too difficult to administer or because they cause rich people to 
flee. Previous attempts have proved unworkable. In 1990, 12 rich 
countries taxed fortunes; today just four do.
A more fundamental overhaul is needed. Wealth should be taxed 
                   /ˈəʊvəhɔːl/彻底改革，大修
more via capital-gains taxes, which currently overpenalise normal
        资本利得（税）
returns and undertax supernormal ones. A start would be to 
introduce a higher headline rate and allowances that exempt 
normal returns.
At the same time, the burden on labour should lighten. That would
be a hedge against disruption from AI, and remove an obstacle to 
                                                     /ˈɒbstəkl/障碍，阻碍
employment in countries where labour taxes are crushing.
In Belgium they constitute 52% of the costs of employing a person
. Consumption should be taxed more heavily, with a focus on 
ending the litany of exemptions. That would have the useful 
                     /ɪɡˈzempʃnz/（税额）豁免，减免项
side-effect of taxing the spending of the very rich.
This might not curb the influence Elon Musk can buy, but it would
               /kɜːb/抑制，限制
ensure the state gets a bigger cut when Mr Musk buys another 
plane. Finally, codes should be radically simplified. None of 
this will be easy.
The losers will hiss more loudly than the winners will cheer. But
change is coming, like it or not. The country that gets ahead of 
the hurricane will one day thank the politicians who had the 
foresight to act.
