Inside the Nobel Prize in literature

Alfred Nobel was a keen but mediocre writer. The Swedish dynamite
                            /ˌmiːdiˈəʊkə/平庸的，二流的
tycoon wrote unpublished poems and a play featuring a 40-minute 
/taɪˈkuːn/大亨，巨头
torture scene.
 
To the dismay of relatives keen to inherit his fortune, his 
 
handwritten will of 1895 endowed five awards, including a prize 
                         /ɪnˈdaʊd/捐赠设立（奖项、基金）
for literature.
 
Nobel instructed "the Academy in Stockholm" to give it to the 
 
writer of "the most outstanding work in an ideal [idealisk] 
 
direction". The Swedish Academy spent decades puzzling over what 
 
"ideal" meant, before quietly dropping the criterion.
                                           /kraɪˈtɪəriən/标准，准则
Next Thursday, a door will open in a gilded hall in Stockholm and
                                     /ˈɡɪldɪd/镀金的，富丽堂皇的
the Academy's formally dressed permanent secretary will step 
 
forward to announce, in Swedish and English, the winner of this 
 
year's literature Nobel.
 
Now prediction markets favour Indian writer Amitav Ghosh, the 
 
Chinese avant-garde author Can Xue or the American novelist 
        /ˌævɒ̃ˈɡɑːd/前卫派的，先锋的
Thomas Pynchon to win the SKr12mn (£910,000) in prize money — 
 
though nobody really knows, as today's Academy is less leaky than
 
it was before a #MeToo scandal prompted a sweeping overhaul.
                                          彻底的、大范围的（≠清扫的）
And the pundits usually guess wrong. The prize is ritually mocked
        /ˈpʌndɪts/权威评论员，行家/ˈrɪtʃuəli/例行公事般地，惯常地
. The Nigerian poet Chinweizu Ibekwe once marvelled at "the 
 
conceit that a gaggle of Swedes... should pronounce on 
/kənˈsiːt/自负，自高自大/ˈɡæɡl/（贬义）乌合之众，一群
intellectual excellence for the whole wide world".
 
And yet, says Daniel Medin, professor of comparative literature 
 
at the American University of Paris: "The Nobel is the big one 
 
for international fiction. There is no comparison."
 
The Nobel shapes the global canon — even if the globe can push 
                            /ˈkænən/经典作品，正典
back. There has always been a comic discrepancy between the 
                                    /dɪsˈkrepənsi/不一致，差异
weight of the prize and the parochialism of the judges.
                            /pəˈrəʊkiəlɪzəm/目光狭隘，地方本位
The Academy's 18 life members — known as "The Eighteen" — are 
 
Swedish writers, literary historians, linguists and a jurist, 
                                                      /ˈdʒʊərɪst/法学家，法理权威
some of them obscure even in Sweden. There were barely any female
             默默无闻的（≠模糊难懂的）
members until this century.
 
The very first award, in 1901, made international news. Somehow 
 
nobody nominated Leo Tolstoy, whereas 17 members of the Academie 
 
Francaise endorsed the French poet Sully Prudhomme, and he won.
          公开支持、力挺（≠在背面签名）
The literature prize has never been as controversial as the 
 
frankly political Peace Prize; but because greatness in 
 
literature is more subjective than in the sciences, the Academy's
                   /səbˈdʒektɪv/主观的
choice reliably angers critics.
       总是、照例地（≠可靠地）
They delight in drawing up lists of overlooked greats — James 
               起草，列出（名单）
Joyce, Franz Kafka, Virginia Woolf, Marcel Proust, Constantine 
 
Cavafy, et cetera — though some of these achieved renown only 
 
after death.
 
The prize was at its most politicised during the cold war, when 
 
the Academy chose the unreliable Soviet writer Boris Pasternak 
 
(1958) and dissidents Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn (1970) and Joseph 
           /ˈdɪsɪdənts/持不同政见者，异见者
Brodsky (1987).
 
Pasternak initially accepted the nobelevka, as the award is 
 
popularly known in Russia — but then, under pressure from the 
 
regime, rejected "this undeserved prize which has been presented 
 
to me".
 
Jean-Paul Sartre, who refused the Nobel in 1964, noted among his 
 
reasons that the prize was "reserved for the writers of the West 
 
or the rebels of the East." By the 1980s, the Academy — earlier 
 
than most of the Western literary establishment — began looking 
 
beyond the west.
 
A "Nobel committee" of four or five academicians whittles down 
 
the nominations to a shortlist of five, which the Eighteen spend 
                     /ˈʃɔːtlɪst/决选名单，入围名单
the summer reading. They decide in early October. Translation has
 
become central to the award.
 
The Nobel has helped make the international canon more inclusive.
                                            /ˈkænən/经典作品，正典
After going global, the prize has recently become gender-equal. 
 
This followed a #MeToo scandal in 2017, when 18 women accused the
 
French husband of Academician Katarina Frostenson of sexual 
 
assault and rape.
 
He was later jailed. Several members quit — although the body's 
 
rules forbid resignation — and the post-scandal "Eighteen" 
 
included many more women.
 
They awarded the 2018 prize, a year late, to Polish writer Olga 
 
Tokarczuk, and there have been three female laureates since 2018.
 
In fact, notes Kaj Schueler, author of a Swedish book on the 
 
prize, there's been a precise pattern: male and female victories 
 
alternate.
 
If that continues, then after Krasznahorkai's win in 2025, this 
 
year's laureate would be a woman, perhaps it will be Xue's year? 
       /ˈlɒriət/（桂冠）获奖者
The winners' reactions are part of the award's lore.
 
John Steinbeck in 1962, when asked whether he deserved the Nobel,
 
replied, "Frankly, no." Many critics agreed.
 
Samuel Beckett (1969) was on holiday in Tunisia with his wife 
 
Suzanne when a telegram from his French publisher arrived: "In 
 
spite of everything, they have given you the Nobel Prize. I 
 
advise you to go into hiding."
 
Suzanne, when told the news, exclaimed, "Quelle catastrophe." 
 
Beckett dubbed December 10, the annual date of the award ceremony
 
, "Nobloodybeldamday".
 
Bob Dylan, winner in 2016, ignored the Academy's phone calls and 
 
didn't acknowledge the prize for over a fortnight.
                                        /ˈfɔːtnaɪt/两星期，十四天
