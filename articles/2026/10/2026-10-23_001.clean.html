How a Sydney professor built an AI army to scam the scammers

The day that Dali Kaafar decided to start a company that unleashes an autonomous army of AI bots to take on the trillion-dollar global scam industry began like any other.

It was a warm Saturday afternoon in spring. The Macquarie University professor of cybersecurity was enjoying a picnic with his wife and two children at a Sydney park. At some point, his mobile rang.

Kaafar could tell from the number that it was a scam call, and while he usually ignored them, on this particular day, Kaafar decided to have some fun with the fraudsters.

He picked up the call.

"The kids were a few years younger then, and for their entertainment, I pretended not to understand what the scammers were after, or I would give them the wrong details," he says.

The scammers persisted, insisting that there was something wrong with Kaafar's internet and they needed remote access to his computer. Kaafar feigned incomprehension when they spoke about his tepid upload and download speeds.

He distracted and delayed them: what was this NBN they spoke of? "At one point, I think I was even trying to order a pizza," he says.

"As I hung up that call I remember very clearly saying, 'this was all great fun, but imagine what technology can do with these scammers at scale,'" he says. While his children were in hysterics, his wife was less impressed.

"My wife said, 'those are 44 minutes of your own life that you will never get back.'

You ruined the whole picnic experience. I said, 'That's even more reason for technology to jump in to do it better.'" The following Monday, Kaafar called together a cohort of PhD students and a postdoctoral researcher with expertise in AI security and natural-language processing.

They would build voice bots to ape the tech insecurities of the average caller: umm-ing and ahh-ing their way through conversations with scammers and apologising for not being very good with technology or computers.

"But then we all immediately realised that by keeping the scammers on the line we can also extract and deliver this real-time intelligence, actionable intelligence, to stay on top of what scammers are doing." In 2024, Kaafar founded Apate.ai, named after the Greek goddess of deception and fraud.

Apate.ai deploys thousands of digital decoy identities across phone, email, messaging apps and social platforms, designed to be discovered directly by scammers searching for victims.

"We are flipping the script on the economics of fraud by beating these bad actors at their own game." Crucial to the success of the fleet of Apate.ai bots is their believability: whether texting or talking, Apate.ai agents must resemble an actual person – rather than a minutely programmed chatbot – to keep scammers on the line.

The agents have phone numbers and WhatsApp accounts; they have personalities, PayIDs and pet peeves. Some have distinctive accents; others employ broad Australian slang ("no worries", they may say or "let me just get a cuppa").

"We used to give them cute names, like Ricky and Pete, and we each had our favourite. But as we evolved to more than 200,000 bots, now they just have ID numbers," says Kaafar.

Each bot operates with a defined mission, which may include keeping a scammer talking for as long as possible, extracting crucial intelligence about the scammers' operations, or manoeuvring the fraudster onto another platform where the interaction can continue.

The mission may result in gathering details of scammer impersonation tactics, phishing URLs, mule bank accounts and crypto wallets – crucial information to help authorities shut down these networks.

It's not unusual for Apate.ai voice bots to keep scammers on the line for more than two hours, with the record at two hours and 12 minutes at the time of our interview.

"Literally a scammer is busy talking to a bot is a scammer that is not placing thousands of calls a minute to other potential victims," says Kaafar.

Occasionally, scammers end the call because they cotton on that they are talking to a bot. The realisation that a bot has been wasting the scammer's time usually results in a slew of profanities.

"I think we're the only company around that has a KPI linked to f-words," he says. Kaafar is keen to emphasise that while delaying and obfuscating scammers is deeply satisfying, it offers benefits beyond merely blocking calls.

"What we're trying to do is distract them and disrupt them by presenting them with the perfect victim.

Then we make it really painful for them where exactly it matters for them, which is how much money they'll be making. "We want to put the foot on the gas," he says.
