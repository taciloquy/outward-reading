The Great Louvre Robbery Was Just the Start. It's Been the Year of the Heist.

In the year since thieves stunned the world by smashing their way into the Louvre Museum and stealing France's crown jewels worth at least $101 million, art heists have only intensified.

From Wales to Sicily, more than 14 museums have been hit by smash-and-grab criminals since early 2025, suggesting museums haven't cracked the code on how to safely protect their treasures. Police are still looking for the Louvre's stolen trove of 19th-century jewelry.

Experts have myriad theories about why art heists are increasing, from soaring gold prices making golden antiquities and precious gems more valuable to the misguided notion that stolen art can reap higher sums on the black market—it cannot.

But museum directors and security firms agree the Louvre breach set an ominous precedent. "Louvre was a wake-up call that anywhere can be hit," said Chris Marinello, chief executive of Art Recovery International.

For its part, the Louvre created a new security coordinator position now held by Olivier Goupil, a veteran police officer. The museum also added more crowd-control barriers and anti-ram devices around its exterior.

There are now window railings at the upper-level Apollo Gallery where thieves broke in. In a statement, the museum said the challenge is to balance its mission of protecting its collection while also sharing it with roughly 30,000 visitors a day.

"Museums are not fortresses," the museum said in an email.

The Louvre heist revealed that thieves are increasingly stealing gold, bronze and jewels more than traditional paintings—a turnaround from a century ago when a thief stole its "Mona Lisa," later recovered.

In 10 of the subsequent museum break-ins across Europe over the past year, thieves bypassed paintings to steal objects that could be dismantled or melt down, because such commodities retain value as precious metals and gems.

Black-market jewelers are often willing to re-cut and reshape bigger jewels into smaller untraceable ones that can be resold anywhere, investigators say. On Oct. 5, thieves broke into the C. Karoutzos Contemporary Art Center in central France and hauled off a 330-pound bronze book cover decorated with gold, gems and shells designed by surrealist Salvador Dalí.

Museum owner Christian Karoutzos said his video cameras captured a few shadowy figures leaving the scene, but the book cover remains missing. On the other hand, authorities have had better success recovering stolen paintings.

It likely helped that the works were identifiable and only valuable if left intact, Marinello said.

"You can't melt down a Renoir." In 1990, two men disguised as police officers spent 81 minutes crisscrossing Boston's Isabella Stewart Gardner Museum to cut out and carry off masterpieces by Vermeer and Rembrandt.

This year's heist at the Magnani Rocca took less than three minutes. Geoff Kelly, a former FBI agent, said thieves are breaking in, grabbing what they can and fleeing the scene within minutes—before the police arrive.

In July, Paris's Museum of Natural History suffered an undetected cyberattack that disabled its alarm and surveillance systems, police said.

Two months later, robbers monitoring the digital breakdown were able to saw through an emergency exit door and use blowtorches and other weapons to steal about 13 pounds of gold worth around $2 million.

An earlier audit of the museum's security protocols found that the Louvre's video surveillance and physical security systems relied on a weak password, "LOUVRE." It was immediately changed, the museum said.

Marinello said thieves are beginning to use artificial intelligence to identify museums with hefty holdings of gold or those housed in smaller, historic buildings that might be easier to breach.

It's "something that should scare the bejesus out of museum directors," he said.

As an experiment, Marinello asked an AI tool to help him steal a painting; the tool refused to participate in a possible crime but still assisted him in locating rope and sledgehammers and other gear from the nearest hardware stores.

The flip side: Museums say they're using AI-assisted video surveillance to help guards scour for bad actors by analyzing people's behavior as they move through the galleries rather than recording footage passively.

Victimized museums are often compelled to cope by closing indefinitely or reopening with fewer treasures on view.

The Louvre has reopened its Apollo Gallery, but all the remaining jewelry in it has been removed, resulting in an empty, ceremonial 17th-century gallery.

At the Louvre, staff are still discussing when and how to put the theft's lone survivor back on public view: Empress Eugenie's 19th-century crown, which thieves dropped and crumpled outside the museum during their getaway.

Conservators say one of its eight golden eagles is missing and so are 10 of its 1,354 diamonds, but its 56 emeralds and central globe survived.

"It will be exhibited at the Louvre when we have found the ideal location to create a dedicated, secure exhibition space," the museum said, "but for now, not all conditions are in place."
