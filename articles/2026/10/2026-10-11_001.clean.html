Superstar podcasts are Silicon Valley's way of talking to itself

EACH WEEKDAY morning Jordi Hays and John Coogan, two entrepreneurs-turned-podcasters, enter a sauna in Los Angeles with copies of the Wall Street Journal. They come out with jackets, ties and hot takes on the tech news of the day.

Then they go on air as hosts of the "Technology Business Programming Network" ("TBPN") podcast, riffing with guests, banging gongs and blowing airhorns. Silicon Valley laps it up.

It is not uncommon to see "TBPN", rather than CNBC, streaming on the TV screens in tech firms' offices. Podcasts are the latest example of a power law in Silicon Valley. Only a few become big hitters.

Those that do attract the demigods of tech as guests, making them even more influential. "TBPN" has interviewed Sam Altman of OpenAI and Mark Zuckerberg of Meta.

The "All-In" Podcast, hosted by four plutocrats who straddle tech and MAGA politics, held a summit in Los Angeles in September attended by Elon Musk and Jensen Huang.

(When Donald Trump phoned Nvidia's boss on stage during the summit, Mr Huang put the president on speakerphone.) All share an in-crowd mystique bordering on incestuousness.

In April OpenAI reportedly spent $100m-200m to buy then-18-month-old TBPN (though there was no mention of the affiliation during Mr Altman's interview). The "All-In" hosts are self-described "besties" with many of their billionaire interviewees.

They are also an echo chamber, with a tin ear at times to the concerns of the rest of America. This new podcasting elite reflects a big change in Silicon Valley's relationship with the media.

During the last great investment boom, the dotcom era of the 1990s, publications like Wired were tech-industry gospels.

They covered the internet like Rolling Stone wrote about rock'n'roll, says John Battelle, a founding editor of Wired: "We thought we were covering the counterculture." But the rise of social media and the growing power and wealth of big tech generated a rift between Silicon Valley and news outlets.

Podcasters, who share their guests' techno-optimism, provide a friendlier platform. The watershed came during the 2024 presidential race. Led by Mr Trump, politicians saw the medium as a less filtered way of getting their message out than the press or broadcast TV.

Two of its billionaire co-hosts, David Sacks and Chamath Palihapitiya, threw a fundraiser in San Francisco for Mr Trump. Shortly afterwards he appeared on their show.

That helped raise the profile of the hosts, as well as of tech podcasting in general. Helping them thrive is what would be called, in Silicon Valley jargon, a flywheel.

Just before he hit the big time in 2024, Mr Patel tweeted his bank balance. It showed savings of $0.14. Then Mr Zuckerberg came onto the show and the advertising dollars poured in.

As more prominent executives take part, not only do advertisers notice; so do potential customers, investors and jobseekers, raising the quality of the audience.

"This is luxurious media with very valuable eyeballs," says Josh Kaplan, boss of Smooth Media, which represents podcasters. The format is particularly well-suited to Silicon Valley, where founders invent stories for a living.

"We're not journalists; we are enthusiasts," says Patrick O'Shaughnessy, whose podcast, "Invest Like the Best", has a strong following in VC circles.

These offerings may be popular, but do not expect them to invest in the costly investigative work that uncovers frauds, such as that at Theranos, a health-tech startup, exposed by the Wall Street Journal in 2015.

Instead, AI is at the centre of their discussions. Unlike many parts of America, they treat it as almost the only topic worth discussing.

But this obsession has further pushed Silicon Valley into a "monoculture", says David Senra, an influential podcaster. Recently he has been to tech dinners where conversation veers only between AI optimism and doomerism.

"It sounds like they are all reading the same tweets," he says. Lulu Cheng Meservey, a PR consultant to tech founders, reckons nine-tenths of people in tech speak only to other people in tech. Podcasts exacerbate that.

They may be well-meaning, she says, but "when a billionaire AI salesman is telling you whether to buy AI, you are taking that from someone who is entirely unrelatable to you." It may get worse.

Silicon Valley's podcasters sprinkle their conversations with reverence for space missions, robots and other techno-wizardry. In an Alpocalypse, they would come out better off than most people. Mr Battelle calls it the "golden ticket on the rocket ship".

If all else fails, they can podcast from Mars. But will people still want to listen to them?
