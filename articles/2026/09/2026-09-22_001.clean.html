An AI deal with China could get Donald Trump his Nobel prize

Donald Trump is about to have the opportunity of a lifetime—if he is up for it. As OpenAI's Sam Altman and others have noted in recent days, making a deal with China around artificial intelligence could actually win Mr. Trump the Nobel peace prize he has long sought. Where better to seal such an agreement than his one-day summit with the Chinese leader on September 24th?

"We're leading China in AI," he said on September 13th after Mr. Altman and Elon Musk joined Dario Amodei of Anthropic in calling for the pace of AI development to slow to avoid a catastrophe. "We're the most sophisticated country in the world, and frankly I want to keep it that way because whoever wins AI wins."

But the reality is that China has already largely caught up, and the AI race has not worked out particularly well for either country. Incidents such as OpenAI's models attacking Hugging Face, another AI firm, show the threat AI agents can pose to the stability and infrastructure of both countries. If the AI bubble pops, both countries may suffer economically. More Americans are concerned about AI than are excited about it, which has fuelled a growing resistance to data centres, and China has its own growing segment of doomers and sceptics as well.

I've written about global AI policy for the past decade, and continue to speak regularly with key international players. I firmly believe that China would be open to discussing some kind of collaboration in AI. On September 2nd Kyle Chan of the Brookings Institution, who follows the situation closely, wrote that China seems to be signalling that it "is willing to agree to joint restrictions [on AI] with the US as long as the terms…are not unilaterally set by the US", a characterisation I have personally heard dozens of times. It is time to stop casting AI as a winner-take-all, zero-sum race. America and China must instead work together to reconceive AI as a grand collaboration across the globe.

Hawks will raise legitimate concerns. America should not share its crown-jewel intellectual property with China; nobody could realistically expect the two countries to waive the possibility of using autonomous weapons. But pragmatically—and for the best outcomes—the Sino-American relationship around AI should have three strands: one that is adversarial, one that is competitive and one that is collaborative.

On the adversarial side, neither country is, for example, going to want to give up the freedom to use drones in war. And although the world might be better off if there was a mutual agreement to forsake drone warfare, reaching such an agreement is so unlikely that it would be a waste of negotiating energy to try. On the competitive side, both countries will want to be—and should be—free to develop chips that are faster and better. Both will keep trying to make the best, cheapest AI that they can. And both would like to sell their wares. Tussling over export controls might not be the best use of energy in the upcoming meeting.

If the two leaders are open to making progress on the third strand, collaboration, where would a breakthrough be most likely? One promising area is medicine, particularly cancer research. China now contributes quite a bit to developing new cancer treatments. Its pioneering immunotherapy drug Ivonescimab, for instance, outperformed a comparable American drug, Keytruda, in a trial, with patients surviving without observable tumours for about twice as long. By running joint drug trials, and sharing (suitably anonymised) data, the countries could collaboratively run bigger, more accurate studies more quickly, facilitating the development of medical AI worldwide—and thus potentially come up with better candidate drug targets, faster.

All of this could begin with an agreement, formal or informal, between the US and China. There would be difficult details to sort out, such as the sharing of intellectual property, including both in advances made directly in technical AI and for any drugs that might be developed. Restrictions around espionage and mutual monitoring and data exchange would no doubt be tricky, too. But there is a substantial—and growing—incentive for both sides to be reasonable, and to reach a deal.

The alternative is grim. As the author Robert Wright recently put it, "America's preoccupation with 'winning' the AI race with China could well lead to unprecedented catastrophes, even catastrophes on a global scale." Next week's summit is a chance for Mr. Trump to change tack, help defuse AI's most explosive risks, and in doing so secure that cherished Nobel honour. The prize is there for the taking.
