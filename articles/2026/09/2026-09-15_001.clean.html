Tech's New Rich Are Suffering From 'Sudden Wealth Syndrome'

It was the kind of exit every entrepreneur dreams about. Anastasia Koroleva and her husband sold for nine figures: 100% cash on Day 1, zero earnouts, no strings attached. But what followed blindsided the founder. Instead of the soft life she'd expected, Koroleva found herself dealing with years of unease and a painful divorce.

Today, Koroleva explores what happens when net worth removes the need to work on Exit Paradox. Her interviews with the extraordinary wealthy have uncovered a recurring theme: For many, the exit does not feel like a crowning achievement — or, at least, not for long. It can feel more like an existential rupture. Guests describe becoming staggeringly rich using the language of trauma: "recovering from a $500 million sale," "thrashing about for a decade" or drifting in a state of aimless ennui.

Financial hardship defined Michia Rohrssen's earliest memories. At age 4, he remembers watching his mother cry over a failing car they had no money to fix. Money was "this constant source of pain in our lives," he recalls. Rohrssen moved to San Francisco in his 20s and went into startups, co-founding automotive software firm Prodigy in 2015. Then, in 2021, when Rohrssen was 31, Prodigy sold for $110 million.

All his money problems were solved. Rohrssen was elated — and although he never thought money could replace inner work, the existential dread that slowly set in turned out to be darker than the poverty he'd survived. "It's really bad," he says. "When you're broke and things are hard, you have this belief that, well, one day when I sell the company then things can get better. It's actually much harder when you've achieved those financial outcomes and you still feel just as broken. You feel just as inadequate, and you're like, 'But I did the thing? " He traveled around a bit but had little purpose day-to-day beyond waking up to see his wife. "I just died," he says.

There's a term for this phenomenon — "sudden wealth syndrome" — says licensed psychotherapist Annie Wright. One reason is velocity. The time between starting a company and hitting a liquidity event has compressed dramatically since the crypto boom, leaving founders less time to mature alongside their wealth. Wright says another factor gets the least attention but can exact a heavier toll: the moral weight of how the wealth was made. Unlike lottery winnings, a founder's fortune is deeply intertwined with their identity.

That makes the success feel earned — but any subsequent dissatisfaction feels painfully personal. Many fall prey to the arrival fallacy: the assumption that reaching the number, the milestone they'd organized their lives around, would deliver lasting peace. When it doesn't, some jump straight into their next venture — less from inspiration than a need to prove the first success wasn't a fluke.

The money, at a certain size, also stops feeling real. "Up to about $20 million, you're still thinking about, 'How do I use this resource well? " says Walling, the psychologist.

"You kind of watch it and pay attention and make good investments. And then there's a number beyond that where it's kind of like Monopoly money. That can have this delusional effect on someone's psychology. They are unmoored and unanchored to the value of anything. "

Finding that equilibrium can take years. Of roughly 1,200 post-exit stories Koroleva has studied, she estimates that a decade after reaching "post-economic" status, 15% are "in a pretty sad place"; 70% are comfortable but feel some emptiness; and just 15% are thriving.

Walling says the people who handle a windfall best tend to hold two ideas at once: "I worked really hard, and I got really lucky. " Reward without inflated self-regard. Those who struggle most tend to be the ones for whom the company was everything — no healthy relationships, no hobbies, no sense of meaning outside the exit. "That's where you tie your whole self-worth and your individual sense of well-being to this event," Walling says. No amount of money is "going to be enough to sustain that for you. " For now, these are problems for the very few and the very rich. But some thinkers believe AI could make the scenario universal.

A harder view is that there is no lasting fix. Staying dissatisfied is how our species has survived, why yesterday's luxury reliably becomes tomorrow's basic need.
